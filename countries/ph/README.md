Most farming rules are national, but your city or municipality (the LGU) decides a lot about how they play out. Zoning, business permits, sanitary permits and animal limits are all local. Call your municipal or city agriculture office before you buy land or animals.

## Planning and zoning

- Every city and municipality has a land use plan and a zoning ordinance. Find out how your parcel is zoned before you build or bring in animals.
- Turning farmland into residential, commercial or industrial land takes three separate approvals. DAR approves the conversion, the LGU reclassifies the land in its land use plan, and DENR certifies the conversion is ecologically sound.
- Some LGUs ban poultry or livestock in residential zones, or require a minimum distance from neighbors. There are national siting rules for poultry and livestock farms too. <!-- TODO: needs source: exact DA administrative order and distances -->

## Registering animals

- **Animal facilities.** The Animal Welfare Act (RA 8485, amended by RA 10631) lists poultry farms, hog farms, goat farms and stock farms, among others. DILG Memorandum Circular 2016-12 tells LGUs to ask for BAI registration before they issue a business permit for them.
- **Backyard animals.** A few hens for your own eggs usually don't need a national poultry farm permit, but your LGU can set its own limits or ask you to register. - **Bird flu.** BAI issues rules on moving live poultry and poultry products during avian influenza outbreaks. Read the current memorandum circulars on <https://www.bai.gov.ph/> before you move any birds.

## Selling food

- **Business permits.** To sell food as a business you'll generally need a barangay clearance, DTI registration (sole proprietor) or SEC registration, a sanitary permit from the city or municipal health office, and the mayor's business permit.
- **Processed or prepackaged food.** If you make, repack or distribute it, you usually need an FDA License to Operate and a registered product. A small outlet selling unpackaged food, like a carinderia, generally doesn't need the FDA license.
- **Meat.** Slaughterhouses, poultry dressing plants and similar establishments need an NMIS License to Operate under the Meat Inspection Code (RA 9296). To sell dressed meat, the animal has to go through a licensed facility. <!-- TODO: needs source: rules for small local sales in wet markets -->

## Subsidies and grants

Don't build a small farm's plan around subsidies. Most of the money goes to registered farmers, and most of that goes to rice and corn.

- **RSBSA.** Sign up at your municipal or city agriculture office for the Registry System for Basic Sectors in Agriculture. Being listed is required for many programs, including RCEF and the free crop insurance.
- **RCEF (Rice Competitiveness Enhancement Fund).** For rice farmers. It pays for certified seeds, machinery, credit and training. The original 2019 Rice Tariffication Law (RA 11203) gave it six years. RA 12078, signed on 9 December 2024, extended it to 2031 and raised the yearly budget from ₱10 billion to ₱30 billion. Farmer cooperatives and associations can apply for accreditation.
- **PCIC crop insurance.** Since 2026, the cover for rice and corn under PCIC's multi-peril insurance is ₱25,000 per hectare, up from ₱20,000. That is the most it pays for a total crop loss from insured causes like typhoons, pests and disease. Other product lines and other programs have different amounts. <!-- TODO: needs source: PCIC cover limits for other crops, livestock, and for borrower or self-financed farmers --> Free coverage reaches rice, corn, high-value crops, fisheries, livestock and non-crop assets for farmers listed in the RSBSA. You can also pay premiums and insure on your own.
- **Credit.** ACPC runs credit programs with Land Bank. One example is SURE Aid, which gave rice farmers on one hectare or less a ₱15,000 zero-interest loan in 2019. <!-- TODO: needs source: which credit programs are open today -->

## Tax notes

- **Income tax.** The first ₱250,000 of annual taxable income is exempt. After that, graduated rates apply.
- **The 8% option.** If you're self-employed, not VAT-registered, and your gross sales stay at ₱3,000,000 or below, you can pay a flat 8% on gross sales above ₱250,000 instead. It replaces both the graduated income tax and the percentage tax. Choose it on your first quarterly return, or file BIR Form 1905 at the start of the year. Once you pass ₱3 million you go back to graduated rates and VAT.
- **VAT.** The rate is 12% and the threshold is ₱3,000,000. Agricultural food products sold in their original or simply processed state are VAT-exempt under Section 109(A) of the Tax Code.
- **Percentage tax.** If you only sell raw or simply processed farm products, you don't owe the 3% percentage tax. Section 116 only catches people who are VAT-exempt because their sales fall under the ₱3 million line (Section 109(CC)). Farm products are exempt under a different provision, Section 109(A), so they're outside both VAT and percentage tax. If you also sell other things, like resold goods or services, those sales can still be taxed, so ask your Revenue District Office (RDO) how it treats your mix.
- **Receipts and invoices.** Under BIR Revenue Regulations 12-2023, agricultural producers with gross sales of ₱1,000,000 or less for the year don't have to issue receipts or invoices for their agricultural food products. You still keep a simple sales book: date, what you sold, amount, any tax withheld, what you actually received, and the buyer's name, address and TIN when the buyer is a business. The exemption only holds while farm products are the bulk of your sales, with other sales at no more than 30% of the total.
- **Real property tax (land tax).** Your LGU collects it on land, and the assessor works it out in three steps:
  1. Take the market value of the land.
  2. Multiply it by the assessment level to get the assessed value.
  3. Multiply that by the tax rate.

  Section 218 of the Local Government Code caps the assessment level for land at 40% for agricultural, 50% for commercial and industrial, and 20% for residential. Your LGU's tax ordinance sets the real level at or below those caps, so agricultural land usually costs less to hold than commercial land. It can be well below 40%. Tuguegarao City's 2020 ordinance, for example, uses 17% for agricultural land. The basic tax rate is up to 1% in provinces and up to 2% in cities and Metro Manila municipalities. On top of that there's an extra 1% for the Special Education Fund. Many LGUs give a discount for paying early.

  Worked example, using made-up numbers: land with a market value of ₱500,000 in a province, assessed at the 40% cap, comes to an assessed value of ₱200,000. The basic tax at 1% is ₱2,000 and the Special Education Fund at 1% is ₱2,000, so about ₱4,000 a year. At a lower level like 17%, the assessed value would be ₱85,000 and the bill about ₱1,700. Ask your municipal assessor for the actual level and rate. <!-- TODO: add the local assessment level and rate for a real LGU, with year -->
