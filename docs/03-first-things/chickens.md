# Chickens (the hello world of livestock)

> Your first animals. Small, cheap, and they pay rent in eggs.

## What it is

You keep a small flock of laying hens. Three to six is a good start. They
live in a coop at night and a run or garden by day.

Every day you let them out, feed them, give fresh water, collect eggs, and
shut them in at dusk. Once a week you clean the coop.

You get eggs, manure for compost, and practice at daily animal care. You do
not need a cockerel for eggs.

**Running costs:** a laying hen eats about 120 g of feed a day. That is about
44 kg a year, or a bit over two 20 kg bags per hen. Add bedding, grit, and
mite treatment. See the worked example in [Money](../05-money.md).

## Time per week

- Daily: 10 to 15 minutes. Morning let-out, feed, water, eggs. Evening
  lock-up.
- Weekly: 20 to 30 minutes to clean the coop.
- A few times a year: a deep clean and a mite check.
- Holidays: someone must come every day. An automatic door helps but does not
  replace a person. Many neighbours will do it for the eggs.

## Time to first output

- Hens are sold at "point of lay", about 16 to 20 weeks old.
- First eggs come at about 18 to 22 weeks old. So 2 to 6 weeks after you buy.
- A good hybrid lays about 300 eggs in her first year. Pure breeds lay fewer,
  often 150 to 250.
- Laying drops in winter. Hens need about 14 hours of light to lay well.
- Laying falls after 2 to 3 years. Hens can live 5 to 8 years or more. Decide
  now what you will do with old hens.

## What you need

- **Space:** the free-range standard in the UK and EU is 4 m² of outdoor
  space per hen. More is better. Small runs turn to mud.
- **Coop:** dry, with air flow but no draughts. Perches to sleep on and nest
  boxes to lay in. <!-- TODO: needs source for perch length per bird and hens per nest box -->
- **Predator proofing:** foxes dig, climb, and come in daylight. Use strong
  weld mesh, not chicken wire. Bury a mesh skirt or use electric netting.
- **Water:** fresh every day. It freezes in winter.
- **Feed:** layers pellets, plus grit and a source of calcium for shells.
- **Skills:** none to start. Learn to check a hen for mites and to spot a
  sick bird. A sick hen stands hunched and still.
- **Legal bits:** many countries make you register poultry, even a few. Bird
  flu rules can force you to keep hens under cover for months. Some housing
  rules and deeds ban poultry. See your [country page](../../countries/README.md).

## Common mistakes

1. **Trusting chicken wire.** It keeps hens in. It does not keep foxes out.
   Use weld mesh and lock up every night.
2. **Forgetting lock-up once.** A fox needs one night. An automatic door on a
   light sensor or timer is worth the money. Still do a head count.
3. **Buying a tiny coop.** Sellers overstate capacity. A "6 hen" coop often
   suits 3. Buy bigger than the label says.
4. **Ignoring red mite.** They hide in the coop by day and feed at night.
   Check the perch ends with a white tissue. Treat early.
5. **Leaving feed out overnight.** Rats arrive. Use a treadle feeder or take
   the feed in.
6. **No plan for sick or old hens.** Vets who treat hens are rare and cost
   more than the hen. Learn how to put a bird down, or know who will do it.
7. **No plan for a housing order.** During bird flu outbreaks you may have to
   keep hens under a roof. A covered run saves a lot of stress.

## Good for / Not good for

**Good for:**

- Your first animals.
- Families. Children can do most of the jobs.
- People who are home every morning and evening.
- Gardeners. The manure makes good compost.

**Not good for:**

- People who travel often with nobody to cover.
- Tiny gardens with close neighbours. Hens are fairly quiet. Cockerels are
  not.
- Saving money on eggs. It rarely works out. See [Money](../05-money.md).
