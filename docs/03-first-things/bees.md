# Bees (40,000 workers, no stand-ups)

> A distributed system you do not control. Great for small spaces. Needs training.

## What it is

You keep one or more colonies of honey bees in hives. A strong colony has
tens of thousands of bees in summer. You do not feed them daily. You inspect
them, give them space, manage disease, and stop them leaving.

In spring and summer you open each hive about once a week. You check for a
laying queen, enough food, enough room, signs of disease, and signs of
swarming. In winter you mostly leave them alone.

You get honey, wax, and better pollination nearby. You also get stung.

## Time per week

- Spring to mid summer: one inspection per hive about every 7 days. Allow 20
  to 30 minutes per hive once you know what you are doing. Longer at first.
- Late summer: harvest, then varroa treatment and feeding.
- Winter: a quick check now and then. Very little work.
- Holidays: winter is easy. In swarm season, two weeks away can cost you half
  the colony.

## What you need

- **Space:** a few square metres. Hives work in gardens, on roofs, and on
  allotments where allowed. Face the entrance at a hedge or fence so bees fly
  up above head height.
- **Forage:** flowers within a few kilometres. Bees fly a long way, so your
  own garden matters less than the wider area.
- **Water:** a shallow source nearby, or they will use the neighbour's pond.
- **Equipment:** hive, suit, smoker, hive tool, feeder. Borrow an extractor
  from your local association.
- **Skills:** take a course before you buy bees. Join a local association.
  A mentor in year one is worth more than any book.
- **Legal bits:** some countries make you register hives. Some bee diseases
  must be reported by law. See your [country page](../../countries/README.md).

## Common mistakes

1. **Buying bees before taking a course.** Learn to read a frame first.
2. **Not managing varroa.** The varroa mite is in almost every colony. If you
   do not monitor and treat, the colony will most likely die.
3. **Missing swarm signs.** Skip inspections in May and June and half your
   bees leave. That also means little honey that year.
4. **Taking too much honey.** The bees starve in winter. Leave enough or feed
   sugar syrup in autumn.
5. **Starting with one hive.** You have nothing to compare it with and no
   spare bees when the queen fails.
6. **Not telling the neighbours.** Talk to them first. A jar of honey helps.
7. **Expecting every colony to survive.** Losses are normal. Surveys of US
   beekeepers have reported yearly colony losses of 30% to 50% in recent
   years.
8. **Not checking for allergy.** A serious sting allergy in the household
   rules this out. Ask a doctor if you are not sure.

## Good for / Not good for

**Good for:**

- Small spaces. No land needed.
- People who like systems, patterns, and careful observation.
- People with a weekly slot of free time, not a daily one.

**Not good for:**

- Anyone with a serious sting allergy at home.
- People who are away for weeks in late spring and early summer.
- Making money at small scale. The kit costs more than the honey for years.
- People who want predictable results.
