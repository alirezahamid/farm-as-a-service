# 05: Money (the business model)

Growing food is the easy half. Selling it at a price that pays you is the
hard half.

This page is general. Tax and food law differ by country. See
[`countries/`](../countries/README.md).

## Unit economics: six hens

A worked example. Change the prices to match your area.

**Assumptions**

| Input | Value | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hens | 6 | |
| Feed per hen per day | 120 g | British Hen Welfare Trust |
| Eggs per hen per year | 280 | Good hybrid, first year |
| Price of a 20 kg bag of layers pellets | £14 | <!-- TODO: needs source --> |
| Sale price for six eggs at the gate | £2.00 | <!-- TODO: needs source --> |
| Your time | 10 minutes a day | |

**The maths**

- Feed per hen per year: 120 g × 365 = 43.8 kg. That is 2.2 bags, or about
  £31.
- Feed for six hens: about £184 a year.
- Eggs: 6 × 280 = 1,680 a year. That is 280 boxes of six.
- Sales, if you sell every egg: 280 × £2.00 = £560.
- Left after feed: about £376 a year.
- Your time: 10 minutes × 365 = about 61 hours.
- Pay per hour: about £6. Before bedding, the coop, the hens, and the eggs
  you eat yourself.

Small scale food production does not beat a salary. It can cover its own
costs and feed you well. Plan around that.

## Pricing (you are not the supermarket)

- You cannot win on price. Supermarkets buy by the truck.
- You can win on freshness, taste, variety, and trust. Price for that.
- Count all your costs. Include packaging, fuel, stall fees, and waste.
- Count your time. If the price does not pay you, the price is wrong or the
  product is wrong.
- Some crops pay and some do not. Salad leaves, herbs, tomatoes, soft fruit,
  and cut flowers earn far more per square metre than potatoes or onions.

## Sales channels (distribution)

| Channel | Good | Bad |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Farm gate or honesty box | No fees. Little time. | Needs passing traffic. Some theft. |
| Farmers' market | Full retail price. You meet customers. | A full day each week. Stall fees. Weather. |
| Veg box or CSA scheme | Money up front. Planned demand. | You must deliver every week, whatever the season does. |
| Your own farm shop | Full margin. Sells other farms' goods too. | Staff, building, opening hours, rules. |
| Selling to shops and restaurants | Bigger, regular orders. | Lower price. They expect steady supply. |
| Online and local delivery | Wide reach. | Packing, delivery time, and platform fees. |
| Wholesale | Sells volume fast. | Lowest price. Rarely works at small scale. |

Selling direct keeps the most money per item. It also costs the most time.
Start with one channel. Add a second when the first sells out.

A CSA (community supported agriculture) scheme means customers pay at the
start of the season for a weekly share of the harvest.

## Rules on selling food (compliance)

The details are local. The pattern is the same in most countries.

- **Vegetables and fruit:** the fewest rules. Usually fine to sell fresh and
  unprocessed.
- **Eggs:** small flocks can often sell direct with few rules. Larger flocks
  need registration and stamping.
- **Honey:** labelling rules apply. Name, weight, origin, and your address.
- **Meat:** must go through a licensed abattoir to be sold. You cannot sell
  meat from an animal killed at home.
- **Milk and dairy:** tightly controlled. Raw milk sales are restricted or
  banned in many places.
- **Processed food:** jam, cheese, baked goods. Usually needs a registered
  kitchen and food hygiene training.
- **"Organic":** a legal term in many countries. You need certification to
  use it.

Check before you sell. Fines cost more than the eggs.

## General tax notes (not tax advice)

- Selling produce is income. Most countries want to know about it, often
  above a small yearly allowance.
- Keep records from day one. Every sale and every receipt. A spreadsheet is
  enough.
- Many countries treat a "hobby" and a "business" differently. A business
  can often set losses and costs against income. A hobby cannot. The test is
  usually whether you run it to make a profit.
- Sales tax or VAT often has special rates for food and a threshold for
  registration.
- Farmland often comes with tax reliefs. These usually need real commercial
  farming, not just owning a field.
- Subsidies and grants count as income.
- Talk to an accountant who has farm clients before you buy land or register
  a business.

## Insurance

- Public liability, for when someone is hurt on your land or by your animals.
- Product liability, if you sell food.
- Many markets ask for proof of both before you get a stall.
- Tell your home insurer if you keep livestock or run a business from home.

## Other income (the pivot)

Many small farms earn more from the farm than from the food.

- Courses and workshops
- Farm stays and camping
- Value-added products, such as jam, cheese, and cider
- Contract work for neighbours
- Writing and video
- Remote tech work, a few days a week. The most common model of all.

Each one is a second business with its own rules and its own time cost.
